# Training Pipeline and Sample Efficiency

## Research Question

Which training-pipeline and learning-budget options should we consider for this local SB3 PPO custom CPU fighting game with a GPU neural network, before choosing between a C rewrite and a GPU engine rewrite?

## Key Sources

### Sample Factory: single-machine actor-learner throughput

- **Source:** [Sample Factory, ICML 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/petrenko20a.html); [architecture docs](https://www.samplefactory.dev/06-architecture/overview/); [sync/async docs](https://www.samplefactory.dev/07-advanced-topics/sync-async/); [configuration docs](https://www.samplefactory.dev/02-configuration/configuration/)
- **Status:** ICML 2020 paper; docs fetched 2026-09-05.
- **What it did:** Split RL training into rollout workers, inference workers, a batcher, and a learner. Data-intensive paths use shared-memory buffers rather than serializing full observations through process messages.
- **Key result/mechanism:** The paper reports more than 100k FPS on demanding 3D environments using an asynchronous GPU-based sampler plus off-policy correction. The docs also describe synchronous mode, where sampling and training alternate, and asynchronous mode, where sampling continues while training runs in the background.
- **Relevance here:** This is the strongest systems pattern for our shape: CPU envs, GPU policy, rollout storage, and parent-worker coordination. The near-term lesson is not "switch libraries tomorrow"; it is to prototype the same dataflow ideas in our runner: grouped envs, batched policy forward, contiguous/shared buffers, and reduced waiting.
- **Risk:** Sample Factory's highest-throughput mode accepts policy lag and uses correction machinery. A synchronous, fixed-policy batched runner can preserve PPO semantics; an asynchronous learner/sampler becomes an algorithm change.

### SEED RL and IMPALA: central inference and policy lag

- **Sources:** [SEED RL, arXiv/ICLR-review version](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06591); [IMPALA, arXiv 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01561)
- **Status:** SEED RL submitted 2019, revised 2020; IMPALA revised 2018.
- **What they did:** SEED RL centralized inference. IMPALA separated actors from learners and introduced V-trace correction for experience generated by older actor policies.
- **Key result/mechanism:** SEED RL reports improved wall-time/cost through accelerated central inference and an optimized communication layer. IMPALA shows high-throughput actor-learner training can remain stable when policy lag is handled explicitly.
- **Relevance here:** Centralized inference itself does **not** have to introduce lag. If rollout collection waits for actions from one current policy version, it is still synchronous PPO-style sampling. Lag appears when workers keep collecting while the learner updates, or when trajectories generated by older weights are trained later.
- **Repo-specific hypothesis:** Combat/self-play may be more affected than navigation. Navigation uses a constant opponent, but combat can load opponent PPO policies per environment. Grouping or centralizing opponent inference could avoid many scattered small model calls. This needs profiling; navigation benchmarks do not prove it.

### PPO/SB3: what can change without changing the algorithm

- **Sources:** [PPO paper, arXiv 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347); [SB3 PPO docs](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/modules/ppo.html); [SB3 vectorized env docs](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/guide/vec_envs.html)
- **Status:** PPO paper v2 from 2017; SB3 docs fetched 2026-09-05 from master docs, while our local benchmark environment used SB3 2.5.0.
- **What they establish:** PPO alternates between environment interaction and several epochs of minibatch optimization over collected data. SB3 exposes the key data-shape knobs: `n_steps`, `batch_size`, `n_epochs`, `device`, and vectorized environments.
- **Relevance here:** Pure runner optimizations should preserve the same rollout data and policy version. Examples: preallocated arrays, fewer worker messages, multiple envs per worker, fixed-policy double buffering, and batched inference. These can be checked with replay equality and throughput.
- **Risk:** Changing history length, frame stack, action repeat, opponent selection, rewards, or rollout/update settings changes the learning problem or optimization dynamics. These need wall-clock time-to-score tests, not only steps/sec.

### What Matters for On-Policy Deep Actor-Critic Methods?

- **Sources:** [Google Research publication page](https://research.google/pubs/what-matters-for-on-policy-deep-actor-critic-methods-a-large-scale-study/); [ICLR 2021 oral page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2021/oral/3534)
- **Status:** ICLR 2021 oral.
- **What it did:** Evaluated more than 50 implementation/design choices in a unified on-policy actor-critic framework, training over 250,000 agents across five continuous-control environments.
- **Key result/mechanism:** The source abstract establishes that low- and high-level implementation choices strongly affect agent performance and are often underdocumented in algorithm papers.
- **Relevance here:** This supports treating "sample efficiency" as its own branch. Model size, batch size, rollout size, advantage handling, frame skip/action repeat, observation normalization, discounting, and optimizer settings can change time-to-quality even when raw FPS is unchanged or worse.
- **Risk:** Their tasks are continuous-control benchmarks, not this fighting game. Use the paper as evidence that these choices matter, not as a direct hyperparameter recipe.

## Synthesis

The practical action space has three buckets.

First, there are **PPO-preserving systems changes**. These keep the game on CPU and keep rollouts generated by one fixed policy version before updating. This includes a production-like GPU re-profile after observation reuse, CPU-inference/GPU-learning comparison for small MLP batches, batched policy forward, multiple envs per worker, preallocated arrays, shared-memory buffers, smaller `info` payloads, fixed-policy double buffering, and improved rollout storage/minibatch gathering. This is the best next bucket because it tests the dataflow shape without rewriting the game or changing the learning target.

Second, there are **algorithm/pipeline changes with policy lag**. Sample Factory async mode, IMPALA-style actor-learner decoupling, and SEED-like continuous acting can keep hardware busier by overlapping simulation, inference, and learning. They also introduce policy-version bookkeeping. If workers collect with stale weights while the learner updates, we need stale-rollout limits, policy-version metadata, and learning-curve validation. This is not automatically wrong, but it is no longer plain SB3 PPO.

Third, there are **learning-budget changes**. In this repo, navigation currently appends zero opponent history and frame-stacks it, so much of the configured input can be zero during navigation. A no-history navigation model, shorter history, smaller MLP, reduced frame stack, tuned rollout/batch settings, changed curriculum/opponent mix, or action repeat may reduce wall-clock time-to-score. These changes should be judged by fixed evaluation suites, not replay equality, because they change what data the agent sees or how often it acts.

For CPU simulation and batched native-env design, see [01-cpu-simulation-and-batching.md](01-cpu-simulation-and-batching.md). For GPU simulation, see [02-gpu-simulation-options.md](02-gpu-simulation-options.md). This note recommends measuring the current pipeline and a fixed-policy batched runner before selecting an engine rewrite. SB3 already batches the learner policy across vector environments; candidate improvements concern batch sizes, scheduling, data handling and separately grouped opponent requests.

## Practical Candidates

1. **Latest production-like GPU profile.** Re-run after observation reuse with 1/8/16 envs, adding higher counts when CPU resources justify them. Include policy-forward timing, rollout-buffer add, minibatch gather, update, parent collection, and worker step. Distinguish CPU-observed intervals from GPU kernel timing. This tests whether the next bottleneck is game logic, inference, storage, or synchronization.
2. **CPU-inference/GPU-learning benchmark.** For small or launch-bound MLP batches, SB3 docs warn PPO/MLP can be CPU-friendly. Test CPU policy forward with GPU or CPU updates separately, because a 4090 can still lose to CPU when batches are too small and launch/transfer overhead dominates.
3. **Synchronous batched runner prototype.** Keep PPO fixed-policy collection. Test 2-4 envs per worker, preallocated result buffers, grouped policy forward, and stable episode accounting against `SubprocVecEnv`.
4. **Trainer data-layout pass.** Profile and optimize rollout storage and minibatch gathering. Earlier diagnostics suggested rollout-buffer batching may matter; a game rewrite would not fix that.
5. **Navigation input/model ablation.** Test no opponent-history during navigation, smaller frame stack, and smaller MLP against time-to-navigation-score. This is a learning change, so success requires evaluation curves.
6. **Combat opponent-inference profile.** In self-play phases, measure whether per-env loaded opponent policies dominate. If yes, grouped centralized inference may help without changing game physics.
7. **Async/APPO experiment only after the above.** If synchronous batching leaves hardware idle, try async collection with explicit policy-version tracking and compare both throughput and score.

## Acceptance Metrics

- **Throughput:** aggregate transitions/sec at production-like settings, warmup excluded, repeated seeds.
- **Pipeline timing:** exclusive time for env step, parent collect, observation transfer, policy forward, action transfer, rollout add, minibatch gather, and update.
- **PPO integrity:** one policy version per rollout for synchronous tests; policy lag and stale-rollout counts for async tests.
- **Behavior:** replay equality for pure systems changes.
- **Learning:** wall-clock time-to-target navigation/combat score for input, model, curriculum, action-repeat, or async algorithm changes.

## Relevance: HIGH

The evidence points to a project-specific hypothesis: our next likely gain is a PPO-preserving pipeline/dataflow prototype, not a full game rewrite. C and GPU simulation remain valid later branches, but they should be selected after the latest profile shows which cost survives batching and trainer-side cleanup.
